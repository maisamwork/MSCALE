What Is the Keyword Density Checker?

Keyword density is the share of a text made up of a particular word or phrase, and it answers two practical questions: is my page actually about what I think it is about, and have I repeated a term so often that it reads like spam? This Keyword Density Checker analyses pasted text in your browser and shows the most frequent one, two and three-word phrases with counts and percentages.

Paste an article, a landing page's text or a draft, and the tool immediately lists the top phrases at each length with visual bars, counts, and density as a percentage of total words. An optional stopword filter hides common words like the and and, so the list surfaces the terms that actually carry meaning.

Writers use it to check focus, editors use it to catch over-repetition, and SEOs use it to confirm a target phrase appears naturally a handful of times rather than mechanically dozens. Because the analysis runs locally, unpublished drafts and client content stay private.

How to Use the Keyword Density Checker

Paste your text into the box, choose whether to filter stopwords, and read the phrase tables for one, two and three-word combinations. Use the percentages to judge focus and spot over-optimisation.

  1. Paste the article or page text into the input area.
  2. Leave the stopword filter on to hide common filler words, or turn it off to see everything.
  3. Review the one-word phrases: your topic words should appear near the top.
  4. Check the two and three-word phrases to see which exact phrases dominate.
  5. Compare densities; a main phrase around one to two percent is a healthy natural level.
  6. Edit your text where repetition is excessive, then re-paste to confirm the balance improved.

How the Keyword Density Checker Works

The analyser tokenises your text into words, normalises case, and counts every contiguous sequence of one, two and three words. Frequencies are then divided by the total word count to produce density percentages. The stopword filter removes a standard list of high-frequency function words before counting, which is what makes the meaningful terms surface instead of drowning under the, and, of and to.

Interpreting the numbers matters more than computing them. A primary keyword near one to two percent density, appearing in the title, first paragraph and a few subheadings, is the classic natural pattern. When a phrase pushes past three or four percent, the text usually starts to read repetitively to humans and pattern-matches to old-school stuffing for search engines, both of which cost you readers.

The multi-word tables are often the most revealing. Two and three-word phrases show what the page really talks about in combinations, which is how modern search engines understand topics too. If the three-word table is dominated by an unintended phrase, the page may be drifting from its intended topic, and that discovery is exactly what this tool is for. All counting happens in your browser, so unpublished work never leaves your machine.

Synonyms and variants are the healthy answer to high density. If a phrase for a running shoes guide climbs past three percent, replacing some occurrences with trainers, footwear or the specific model names lowers the count while broadening the topical coverage, which is precisely how natural expert writing behaves. The checker makes this visible: after a synonym pass, the one-word table spreads across more distinct terms instead of spiking on one.

Word count changes density arithmetic in non-obvious ways. Adding a two-hundred-word section that never mentions your topic dilutes every existing density figure, which can fix borderline over-optimisation without touching a single existing sentence. The reverse is also true: trimming an article for length silently raises the density of everything left behind, so re-check after major edits in either direction.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Pasting HTML with tags instead of the visible text, which pollutes the counts; paste rendered text only.
  • Chasing an exact density number; there is no magic percentage, and natural writing matters more than any target.
  • Reading the unfiltered list and concluding the page is about the and and; always apply the stopword filter first.
  • Removing every repetition of a keyword, which can make the topic less clear to readers and search engines alike.
  • Analysing one paragraph and generalising; density only means something over a full page or article.

Tips, Benefits and Limitations

  • Aim for your main phrase to appear naturally in the title, the opening paragraph and a couple of subheadings.
  • Use the two and three-word tables to discover the phrases your draft actually emphasises.
  • Treat anything above roughly three percent as a signal to vary your wording.
  • Re-run the check after every significant edit; density shifts quickly with rewrites.
  • Compare a high-ranking competitor page's densities to calibrate what natural looks like in your niche.
  • Since analysis is local, use it freely on confidential drafts and client work.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good keyword density?

There is no official target, but one to two percent for a main phrase is a common natural range. Consistency and readability matter more than hitting a number.

What are stopwords and why filter them?

Stopwords are very common function words like the, and and of. Filtering them lets meaningful topic words surface in the rankings instead of grammar words dominating every list.

Does Google use keyword density as a ranking factor?

Not as a simple percentage. Modern search engines understand topics and synonyms, but extreme repetition can pattern-match to spam, which is why the checker is most useful for catching over-optimisation.

Can I check two and three-word phrases?

Yes. The tool reports one, two and three-word phrase tables with counts, density percentages and visual bars for quick comparison.

Is my text uploaded anywhere?

No. All counting and analysis runs in your browser. Nothing you paste is transmitted or stored.

Can I analyse a whole website?

The tool analyses one page's text at a time. Paste each page's content separately to audit a site page by page.
