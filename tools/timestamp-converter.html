What Is the Timestamp Converter?

A Unix timestamp is a count of seconds since January 1, 1970, and it is how almost every computer system records time. It is unambiguous and timezone-proof, which is exactly why it is unreadable: nobody glances at 1735689600 and thinks New Year's Day 2025. This Timestamp Converter translates in both directions, instantly.

Paste a timestamp to convert it to readable dates in UTC and your local timezone, or pick a date and time to get its timestamp. A live clock shows the current timestamp ticking, which is invaluable when debugging time-based logic or setting up scheduled tasks.

Developers use it daily for debugging logs, APIs and cron jobs; analysts use it to make exports readable; anyone configuring systems that speak in timestamps uses it to avoid off-by-a-timezone mistakes. The conversions run entirely in your browser.

How the Timestamp Converter Works

The Unix epoch is midnight UTC on January 1, 1970, and a timestamp counts seconds from that moment, ignoring timezones entirely. Converting to a date therefore has one absolute answer in UTC, and one answer per timezone for local display. Showing both is what prevents the classic bug of comparing a UTC time against a local one.

Modern systems increasingly use millisecond timestamps, a 13-digit count. The tool detects the magnitude of what you paste: 10 digits are treated as seconds and 13 digits as milliseconds, which removes the most common conversion mistake. Dates before 1970 are negative counts and convert correctly too.

In the other direction, a chosen date and time is interpreted in your local timezone, because that is how humans mean time, and its absolute timestamp is derived from that. This is the correct behaviour for scheduling: when you say a meeting is at 9 o'clock, you mean 9 o'clock where you are, and the timestamp captures that absolute instant.

Timezones are the reason timestamps exist, and they are also the source of every timestamp bug, so the model is worth spelling out. A timestamp is one absolute instant, the same everywhere in the universe. A timezone is a display rule that renders that instant as a local clock reading. The conversion bug almost everybody meets once is comparing a local clock reading against a timestamp, or storing a local time as if it were absolute. The fix is always the same: keep absolutes as timestamps, apply timezones only at the edges where humans read or speak times.

Leap seconds are the fine print. The Unix day is defined as 86,400 seconds, but Earth's rotation occasionally demands an extra second, which systems handle by various workarounds. For every practical purpose this noise is invisible; for high-precision systems it is a real discipline with published tables. The tool follows the standard convention, which is what every mainstream system uses.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Storing local times as timestamps, which breaks the moment the data crosses a timezone.
  • Comparing a UTC display string with a local one and concluding time is broken.
  • Assuming every timestamp is in seconds when the API actually speaks milliseconds.
  • Forgetting that date-only values, birthdays and deadlines, are not instants and should not be timestamps at all.

Tips, Benefits and Limitations

  • Ten-digit timestamps are seconds; thirteen-digit ones are milliseconds. The tool detects this automatically.
  • Always check whether a spec means UTC or local time; the timestamp is absolute, the display is not.
  • Use the live clock to capture now when writing time-based tests or examples.
  • Negative timestamps are valid and represent times before 1970.
  • When comparing times from two systems, convert both to UTC first; comparing local strings invites errors.

Frequently Asked Questions

What exactly is a Unix timestamp?

The number of seconds elapsed since 00:00:00 UTC on January 1, 1970, not counting leap seconds. It is the standard way computers store absolute time.

Does it handle millisecond timestamps?

Yes. Thirteen-digit inputs are interpreted as milliseconds automatically, and the conversion accounts for the difference.

Why do UTC and local show different times?

They are the same instant described in two frames. UTC is the absolute reference; local applies your timezone's offset. Both are shown to prevent mix-ups.

Can it convert dates before 1970?

Yes. Timestamps before the epoch are negative numbers and convert correctly to historical dates.

Why does my converted time differ from another tool by hours?

One tool is showing UTC and the other local time, or one assumed a different timezone for the input. Both displays here are labelled to make that distinction explicit.

Is anything uploaded?

No. All conversion happens locally in your browser.

What happens at the 2038 limit?

Systems storing timestamps as signed 32-bit seconds overflow in January 2038. Modern 64-bit systems are unaffected, which is why the industry has been migrating for years.

Why do some timestamps have ten digits and others thirteen?

Seconds versus milliseconds. The magnitude tells you which, and the tool detects it automatically, which removes the most common interpretation error.
