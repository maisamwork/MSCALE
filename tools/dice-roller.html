What Is the Dice Roller?

Physical dice get lost, biased and disputed, and some games need dice nobody owns, like a twenty-sided die or five dice at once. This Dice Roller rolls up to twenty dice with any number of sides, showing each die's result and the total, with rolls that are as fair as software can make them.

Choose the number of dice, choose the sides per die, from classic six-sided to twenty-sided and beyond, and roll. Each die's individual value is displayed alongside the sum, so games that care about individual rolls and games that care about totals both work naturally.

Board game players use it when the physical dice are missing or disputed, tabletop role-players use it for polyhedral dice they do not own, teachers use it for probability demonstrations, and anyone making a quick decision uses it to let chance decide. It runs entirely in your browser with no setup.

How to Use the Dice Roller

Set how many dice to roll and how many sides each has, then roll. Every die's value appears individually along with the total, and rolling again takes one click.

  1. Set the number of dice, from one to twenty.
  2. Set the number of sides per die, such as 6, 10 or 20.
  3. Click roll to throw all the dice at once.
  4. Read each die's individual result and the combined total.
  5. Roll again as often as needed; each roll is independent.

How the Dice Roller Works

Each die is an independent random integer from 1 to its number of sides, generated uniformly so every face is equally likely. The total is simply the sum of the individual values, which are all displayed, so nothing about the roll is hidden.

Independence is the property that makes digital dice fair: no roll is influenced by previous rolls, and every face of every die has the same chance every time. Physical dice develop wear and throwing habits that bias results; digital dice do not, which is why groups sometimes prefer them for important rolls.

The multi-dice display matters for games that read individual dice, not just totals. Role-playing systems check for individual maximums and minimums; probability lessons compare the distribution of individual dice against the bell-shaped distribution of sums. Showing both supports all of these uses without any extra settings.

The distribution of sums is the most instructive thing dice can teach, and the tool's totals make it visible. One six-sided die is uniform: every face equally likely. Two dice summed are not: seven is the most likely total because six combinations produce it, while two and twelve have one combination each. Roll a few dozen times and the humped shape emerges from the totals, which is the central-limit intuition in its simplest form.

Digital dice also settle the fairness disputes that physical dice invite. Worn edges, uneven surfaces and throwing style all bias physical dice in small, real ways. A uniform digital roll has no such history: no throw inherits anything from the last. Groups that argue about dice can simply agree to trust the tool, which removes an entire genre of tabletop negotiation.

Examples and Practical Use Cases

Board game night

The dice are under the sofa and nobody wants to pause the game. Roll two six-sided dice per turn with individual results shown, and the game proceeds without dispute.

Tabletop role-playing

A party needs four twenty-sided dice for a check. Rolling four d20s shows each result individually, which is exactly what the system requires for its rules.

Probability lessons

A class rolls thirty six-sided dice and watches the individual results spread across all faces, then compares the totals against the expected bell curve. More rolls make the pattern clearer.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Reading only the total when the game's rules depend on individual die results.
  • Believing a streak of high rolls means a low roll is due; each roll is independent.
  • Rolling mixed dice sizes in one throw, which this tool does not support; roll each size separately.
  • Forgetting that a single die with two sides is functionally a coin flip.

Tips, Benefits and Limitations

  • Individual die results matter for many games; read them before collapsing to the total.
  • Each roll is independent; streaks mean nothing statistically, however they feel.
  • For a single decision, one die with two sides is a coin flip.
  • Set dice to 1 and sides to your range maximum for a simple uniform pick.
  • Rolling many dice at once is a quick way to demonstrate probability distributions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are the rolls fair?

Each die uses a uniform random source, so every face is equally likely and rolls are independent. Fairness matches or exceeds typical physical dice.

Can I roll different-sized dice together?

This tool rolls dice of one size per throw. Roll each size separately and add the results for mixed pools.

How many dice can I roll at once?

Up to twenty per roll, which covers virtually every game and classroom use.

Can I use it for a d100 roll?

Yes. Set one die with one hundred sides, or roll two ten-sided dice and use your system's percentile convention.

Does it remember previous rolls?

Each roll replaces the display. For record-keeping, copy the results after each roll.

Is anything uploaded?

No. Rolling happens entirely in your browser.

Can I roll percentile dice, a d100?

Yes, as one die with one hundred sides, or two ten-sided dice using your game's tens-and-units convention. The result distribution is identical; the ceremony differs.

Why show individual dice and not just the total?

Many games depend on individual results, such as counting successes or spotting maximums. Showing both supports every rule system without extra settings.

Can I roll for a random order of players?

Roll one die per player with a distinct range per roll, or use the Random Number Generator's unique mode for a straight draw. Both approaches produce a fair, dispute-free order.

Why does the tool cap at twenty dice?

Twenty covers essentially every game system and keeps the individual results readable on one screen. Larger pools are a statistics exercise better served by the Random Number Generator.

Does the roller work offline?

Yes. Once the page has loaded, rolling needs no connection, which makes it reliable in basements, campsites and anywhere table games actually happen.
