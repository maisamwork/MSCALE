What Is the BMI Calculator?

Body Mass Index, or BMI, is a quick screening number that compares your weight to your height. Health services around the world use it to sort adults into broad weight categories, because a single number is far easier to track over time than raw weight alone. This BMI Calculator computes your BMI instantly and shows the category it falls into.

You enter your height and weight, in metric or imperial units, and the tool returns your BMI together with the standard category: underweight, healthy weight, overweight or obese. It also shows the healthy weight range for your height, which turns an abstract number into a concrete target.

BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnosis. It is genuinely useful for spotting trends and for a first conversation about health, but it does not measure body fat directly and it cannot see the difference between muscle and fat. Athletes, older adults and pregnant people need extra context when reading the result, which this page explains below.

How the BMI Calculator Works

The formula is simple. In metric units, BMI equals weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres. Someone who is 170 cm tall and weighs 65 kg has a BMI of 65 / (1.70 x 1.70), which is about 22.5. In imperial units the formula includes a conversion factor of 703: BMI equals 703 x pounds divided by inches squared.

The result is compared against standard adult ranges. A BMI below 18.5 is classed as underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 is the healthy range, 25 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30 or above is obese. These cutoffs come from large population studies linking BMI ranges to health outcomes, and they apply to adults aged roughly 18 to 65.

The healthy weight range shown by the tool is simply the weight that would put you at the edges of the healthy band for your height. It is calculated by rearranging the formula: multiply 18.5 and then 24.9 by your height in metres squared. Seeing the range in kilograms or pounds makes it much easier to set a realistic personal target.

BMI's history explains both its usefulness and its limits. It was devised in the 1830s by the statistician Adolphe Quetelet as a population measure, not an individual diagnosis, and it entered clinical use because it is cheap, repeatable and correlates with body fat at the population level. Those properties remain valuable: no laboratory test is as easy to take repeatedly as height and weight on a scale.

Where BMI misleads is exactly where the population average does not describe the individual. Muscle mass, frame size, age and fat distribution all move the number without moving health in the same direction. This is why modern guidance pairs BMI with a waist measurement, since abdominal fat carries different risk implications than fat stored elsewhere, and why the category bands are brackets rather than verdicts.

Examples and Practical Use Cases

Metric example

A person 165 cm tall weighing 82 kg has a BMI of 82 / (1.65 x 1.65) = 30.1. That falls just inside the obese category, suggesting a conversation with a health professional could be worthwhile and that modest weight loss would move them into the overweight band.

Imperial example

Someone who is 5 feet 9 inches (69 inches) and weighs 150 pounds has a BMI of 703 x 150 / (69 x 69) = 22.1, comfortably in the healthy range. The same person would leave the healthy band above roughly 169 pounds.

Tracking change over time

If your BMI was 27.8 three months ago and is 26.9 today, the number itself matters less than the direction. Recording BMI periodically turns vague intentions into measurable progress, which is one of the main reasons clinicians prefer it to raw weight.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using adult BMI categories for children, who need age and sex specific percentile charts.
  • Measuring height with shoes on or weight at inconsistent times, which shifts the result without any real change.
  • Treating a single high reading as a trend; day-to-day weight fluctuates by a kilogram or more.
  • Ignoring the muscle caveat for athletes and reading a high BMI as automatic proof of excess fat.

Tips, Benefits and Limitations

  • Measure height without shoes and weigh yourself at a consistent time of day for comparable results.
  • BMI reads muscle as fat. Very muscular people can score high while being perfectly healthy.
  • Adult cutoffs do not apply to children, who need age and sex specific charts instead.
  • Where you carry weight matters too; waist measurement adds context that BMI alone lacks.
  • Use BMI as a trend line, not a verdict, and discuss big changes with a professional.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is BMI accurate for athletes?

Not always. Muscle is denser than fat, so muscular athletes often land in the overweight range despite low body fat. For them, measures such as body fat percentage or waist-to-height ratio tell a more accurate story.

Does BMI work during pregnancy?

No. Pregnancy changes weight in ways the formula cannot account for, so BMI categories should not be applied while pregnant. Use pre-pregnancy BMI only where a clinician specifically asks for it.

Are the categories the same for all adults?

The standard ranges apply to most adults, but some health services use adjusted cutoffs for certain ethnic groups because health risks can begin at lower BMIs. Follow your local health guidance where it differs.

What BMI should I aim for?

For most adults the healthy range of 18.5 to 24.9 is the target. If you are outside it, even small movements toward the range are linked to health benefits, so progress matters more than the exact number.

Why does the tool show a weight range?

The healthy range converts the abstract BMI scale back into kilograms or pounds for your exact height. It answers the practical question of what the category means for you personally.

Is my information stored?

No. The calculation happens entirely in your browser. Your height and weight are never sent over the network, stored, or seen by anyone else.

What is a healthy waist-to-height ratio?

Guidance commonly points to a waist under half your height. It is a useful companion to BMI because it captures where weight is carried, which BMI cannot see.

Does age change the healthy BMI range?

The standard 18.5 to 24.9 band applies broadly to adults, but some guidance suggests slightly higher ranges may be acceptable for older adults. Individual factors matter, so discuss targets with a clinician.
