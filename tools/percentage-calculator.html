What Is the Percentage Calculator?

Percentages are everywhere: price cuts at the checkout, exam scores, interest rates, commission deals, battery levels and statistics in the news. Yet the maths behind them is easy to fumble under time pressure, and a small slip can change a price by a lot. This Percentage Calculator takes the guesswork out by handling the most common percentage questions in one place, with instant results as you type.

The tool covers the three calculations people need most often: finding what a given percent of a number is, finding what percentage one number is of another, and measuring the percentage increase or decrease between two values. Instead of remembering whether to multiply or divide, you simply pick the question that matches your situation and enter two numbers.

It is useful for shoppers comparing sale prices, students checking homework, employees reviewing raises or commissions, business owners calculating margins and taxes, and anyone who wants a quick, reliable answer without opening a spreadsheet. Everything runs locally in your browser, so nothing you type is sent anywhere.

How to Use the Percentage Calculator

The calculator is organised into three modes, one for each common question. Choose the mode that matches what you want to know, fill in the two boxes, and the answer appears immediately. There is no calculate button to press.

  1. Pick a mode: percent of a number, what percent X is of Y, or percentage change.
  2. Type the first number, such as the percentage or the starting value.
  3. Type the second number, such as the amount or the new value.
  4. Read the highlighted result, which updates live as you edit the inputs.
  5. Change a value to explore alternatives, for example comparing a 15 percent and a 25 percent discount.

How the Percentage Calculator Works

Each mode applies one simple formula. For percent of a number, the tool multiplies the number by the percentage and divides by 100, so 20 percent of 150 is 150 x 20 / 100 = 30. This matches how percentages are defined: a percentage is a fraction with a denominator of 100.

For the what-percent mode, the tool divides the first number by the second and multiplies by 100. If you scored 42 points out of 50, the calculation is 42 / 50 x 100 = 84 percent. The tool is careful about direction: 42 out of 50 is not the same question as 50 out of 42, and mixing up the order is the most common mistake in percentage maths.

For percentage change, the tool computes the difference between the new and old values, divides by the old value, and multiplies by 100. Percentage change is always measured against the original value, which is why a 50 percent loss followed by a 50 percent gain does not return you to the start: 100 down 50 percent is 50, and 50 up 50 percent is 75.

Direction errors deserve a closer look because they produce plausible-looking wrong answers. When asked what percent 30 is of 150, the correct base is 150, the whole amount. Reversing the inputs gives 500 percent, a number that looks impressive and is simply the answer to a different question. A reliable habit is to say the question in words first, X is what percent of Y, and enter the numbers in exactly that order.

Examples and Practical Use Cases

Shopping discount

A jacket costs 120 and is advertised at 30 percent off. Using the percent-of-number mode, 120 x 30 / 100 = 36, so the discount is 36 and the sale price is 84. Doing this before you reach the till helps you judge whether a deal is genuinely good.

Exam and test scores

You answered 37 questions correctly out of 45. The what-percent mode gives 37 / 45 x 100, which is about 82.2 percent. This is handy for converting raw marks into the percentage scale that schools and courses usually report.

Price and salary changes

Your rent rises from 900 to 990 a month. The percentage-change mode gives (990 - 900) / 900 x 100 = 10 percent. The same mode works for falls: if a subscription drops from 20 to 15, the change is -25 percent, showing the size of the saving.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Dividing by the new value instead of the original when computing percentage change, which understates increases and overstates decreases.
  • Adding successive discount percentages arithmetically instead of applying them one at a time to the shrinking price.
  • Confusing a percentage change with a percentage-point change when comparing two rates.
  • Forgetting to move the decimal point, treating 0.5 percent as 50 percent or the reverse.

Tips, Benefits and Limitations

  • A percentage off is not the same as a percentage of the sale price. 25 percent off 80 is 60, not 80 minus 25.
  • For successive discounts, apply them one at a time. 20 percent then 10 percent off is a 28 percent total reduction, not 30 percent.
  • Percentage change uses the old value as the base, so increases and decreases are not symmetric.
  • Quick mental check: 10 percent of a number is the number with the decimal point moved one place left.
  • When comparing offers, convert everything to percentages of the same base so the comparison is fair.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use negative numbers?

Yes. Negative values are useful for losses, temperature-style changes and decreases. In the percentage-change mode, a negative result simply means the value went down by that percent.

Why does 50 percent off then 50 percent off not make something free?

Because the second discount applies to the reduced price, not the original. Half off 100 is 50, and half off 50 is 25. Two successive 50 percent discounts leave you paying 25 percent of the original price.

What is the difference between percentage and percentage points?

Percentage points describe the difference between two percentages. If a rate moves from 4 percent to 6 percent, it rose by 2 percentage points, but by 50 percent in relative terms. This tool computes relative percentages.

How do I calculate a tip or service charge?

Use the percent-of-number mode. A 15 percent tip on a 46 bill is 46 x 15 / 100 = 6.90. Add that to the bill for the total, or use the dedicated Tip Calculator for per-person splitting.

Does the tool round results?

Results are shown with sensible precision and not silently rounded to whole numbers, so small percentages stay accurate. If you need a specific number of decimal places, round the displayed value according to your own rules.

Is my data sent to a server?

No. All calculations run in your browser with JavaScript. Nothing you type is transmitted, stored or logged, which makes the tool safe for salary, pricing and other sensitive numbers.

What is a basis point?

One hundredth of a percentage point. A move from 4.00 percent to 4.25 percent is 25 basis points. Finance uses basis points because percentage-point talk gets clumsy at small scales.

How do I add a percentage to a number in one step?

Multiply by one plus the rate as a decimal: adding 20 percent is multiplying by 1.20. The percent-of-number mode gives the added part; this single multiplication gives the new total directly.
