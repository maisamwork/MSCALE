What Is the Word Frequency Counter?

What a text actually says, statistically, can be surprising. The words you lean on, the repetition you did not notice, the keywords that dominate a page: all of this is invisible while reading and obvious once counted. This Word Frequency Counter tallies every word in a text and shows the most frequent ones with visual bars.

Paste any text and the tool counts occurrences of every word, lists the top words in order, and displays a bar for each so relative frequency is visible at a glance. Common short words can be filtered out so the meaningful terms rise to the top instead of the, and, of.

The tool is used by writers checking for overused words, students analysing sources, marketers comparing their copy against target keywords, SEO practitioners checking term balance, teachers building vocabulary exercises, and researchers doing quick textual analysis. It runs entirely in your browser, so unpublished drafts stay private.

How to Use the Word Frequency Counter

Paste your text and the frequency table appears immediately, ranked from most to least frequent. Toggle the option to ignore common stop words, and set how many top words to display. The bars make relative frequency obvious without reading any numbers.

  1. Paste or type the text you want to analyse.
  2. Review the ranked list of the most frequent words with their counts.
  3. Enable the stop-word filter to hide very common words like the and and.
  4. Adjust the number of results shown if you want a longer or shorter list.
  5. Use the bars to spot words that dominate disproportionately, then edit your text accordingly.

How the Word Frequency Counter Works

The tool normalises the text before counting: letters are lowercased so that The and the count as one word, and punctuation is stripped so that words followed by commas or periods tally correctly. Each remaining token is tallied, and the totals are sorted by count, with ties broken alphabetically.

Stop words are the high-frequency glue words of a language, such as the, a, in and of. They are usually the top of any raw count while carrying little meaning. The filter removes them from the ranking so content words, the ones that carry your message, are what you see. This single option is the difference between a curiosity and an analysis.

The bars are scaled to the most frequent word in the filtered list, so the top word's bar is full and everything else is proportional. This makes it easy to see whether frequency falls off smoothly, which is typical, or whether one term dominates, which is usually a signal worth acting on.

Beyond spotting overuse, frequency analysis has a comparative mode that is genuinely useful: run the same analysis on two texts and compare the profiles. A draft against its target keyword list shows alignment at a glance. Two chapters by the same author show stylistic consistency. A summary against its source shows whether the summary borrowed the source's emphasis or invented its own. The tool supports all of these because it does one thing, counting, reliably.

Normalisation choices shape the results, and knowing them prevents misreading. Lowercasing merges proper nouns with common nouns, so a text about the company Apple counts apple generously. Singulars and plurals stay separate. These are conventional choices, not errors, but reading the output with them in mind is the difference between analysis and confusion.

Examples and Practical Use Cases

Catching overused words

A writer pastes a chapter and finds relied appearing 14 times, far above any synonym. A targeted editing pass varies the wording, and re-running the counter confirms the fix.

Checking keyword balance

A marketer pastes a landing page and confirms the target keyword appears appropriately without crowding out natural language, keeping the copy readable and the term properly present.

Classroom vocabulary analysis

A teacher pastes a news article and students compare the top content words before and after reading, connecting statistical patterns to the article's actual topic.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Reading the raw list without the stop-word filter and concluding a text is about the word the.
  • Expecting singular and plural forms to merge; they count separately by design.
  • Analysing very short texts, where frequency rankings are noise rather than signal.
  • Forgetting that names and proper nouns count like any other word.

Tips, Benefits and Limitations

  • Always enable the stop-word filter first; raw counts are dominated by glue words.
  • Compare frequency before and after editing to verify that a revision actually varied word use.
  • Singular and plural count separately; words and word are tallied as different terms.
  • Use the bars, not just the numbers, to judge whether a term dominates.
  • For long documents, analyse section by section to see how emphasis shifts across the piece.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are capital letters counted separately?

No. Words are lowercased before counting, so Dog and dog are tallied together.

What are stop words?

Very common function words such as the, a, an, in, and of. They top almost every raw frequency list while adding little meaning, so the tool can filter them out.

Does punctuation affect the counts?

No. Punctuation is stripped before counting, so hello, and hello count as the same word.

Can it analyse other languages?

Yes. Counting works for any language that separates words with spaces. The stop-word list is English-focused, so for other languages read the unfiltered list.

How many top words are shown?

You control the display length, from a handful to a long tail. The ranking itself covers every distinct word in the text.

Is my text uploaded?

No. All counting happens locally in your browser and nothing is transmitted or stored.

Can I export the frequency list?

Select and copy the list directly; the counts are plain text and paste cleanly into spreadsheets for further analysis or charting.

What is a healthy keyword frequency for SEO copy?

There is no magic number and keyword stuffing is penalised. The useful check is balance: the target term should appear naturally, and the frequency profile should look like ordinary language, not repetition.

Does the tool handle hyphenated words?

Yes. A hyphenated compound counts as one word, matching standard word-processor conventions, so well-known is tallied as a single term.

Can I compare two documents' profiles?

Run the tool on each document separately and compare the top lists. Shared high-frequency content words reveal overlap; differing profiles show where the texts diverge.
