Color Palette Generator

Generate tints and shades from any hex color. Click a swatch to copy.

What Is the Color Palette Generator?

Every good colour scheme starts from one colour and grows outward: a darker shade for pressed states, a lighter tint for backgrounds, neighbours that harmonise. This Palette Generator takes any base colour and produces its shades and tints systematically, each one click to copy.

Pick a base colour and the tool builds a full ramp: shades stepping toward black and tints stepping toward white, all sharing the base colour's hue and character. The result is the kind of scale design systems use, where every step has a defined role in an interface.

Designers use it to build product palettes that hold together; developers use it to fill in the missing steps of a design token scale; anyone creating charts, presentations or themes uses it to get a consistent family of colours from a single brand colour rather than improvising.

How to Use the Color Palette Generator

Choose a base colour, and the palette appears immediately: a row of shades darkening below it and tints lightening above it. Click any swatch to copy its value. Adjust the base and the whole ramp follows.

  1. Pick your base colour using the picker or by pasting a value.
  2. Review the generated shades, stepping darker from the base.
  3. Review the tints, stepping lighter from the base.
  4. Click a swatch to copy its colour value.
  5. Adjust the base colour to regenerate the entire ramp around the new colour.

How the Color Palette Generator Works

Shades and tints are defined by lightness. A tint mixes the base colour with white, raising lightness while keeping hue and character; a shade mixes it with black, lowering lightness. The generator steps lightness by fixed intervals, producing an evenly spaced ramp rather than ad-hoc eyeballed steps.

Keeping hue and saturation constant across the ramp is what makes the result feel like a family. Random darker colours rarely relate to their base; systematic lightness changes always do. This is the same principle behind the numbered colour scales in design systems, where each step has a consistent relationship to its neighbours.

Click-to-copy is the workflow feature that matters: building a real palette means moving a dozen values into CSS or a design tool, and friction there adds up. Each swatch copies in standard HEX form, ready to paste without further conversion.

Assigning roles to ramp steps is where palettes become interfaces. A common pattern: the darkest shade for primary text, a mid shade for secondary text, the base colour for primary actions, one step lighter for hover states, and the lightest tints for backgrounds, borders and surfaces. Writing this assignment down once, as a rule, turns every future colour decision into a lookup instead of a debate, which is exactly how design systems earn their keep.

Accessibility belongs in the palette conversation from the start, not as a retrofit. Text must contrast with its background at a ratio that readers with normal vision find comfortable and readers with low vision can still read; the common target is 4.5 to 1 for body text. A ramp makes this checkable: pick the text step and the background step and verify the pair, rather than hoping two ad-hoc colours happen to work.

Examples and Practical Use Cases

Building a brand colour scale

A product's primary colour becomes a full ramp: the darkest shade for text on light backgrounds, mid steps for hover states, the base for primary buttons, and tints for surfaces and highlights.

Filling in missing design tokens

A design system defines steps 100, 500 and 900 but the implementation needs every step. Generating a ramp from the 500 value supplies consistent intermediates instead of guesses.

Chart and data colours

A report needs five related colours for a series. A ramp from one hue gives steps that are clearly related but visually distinct, which is exactly what sequential data needs.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using the base colour for everything instead of assigning ramp roles, which flattens the interface's hierarchy.
  • Choosing steps by eye repeatedly instead of from a systematic ramp, which drifts over time.
  • Ignoring contrast ratios between text and background steps until an accessibility review finds them.
  • Generating a ramp from a colour that already has very low saturation, which produces muddy steps.

Tips, Benefits and Limitations

  • Choose the base colour from your brand or design first; the ramp inherits its character.
  • Use tints for backgrounds and surfaces, shades for text and pressed states.
  • Consistent steps read as intentional; avoid mixing ramp steps with ad-hoc colours.
  • Check contrast between text and background steps for accessibility as you assign roles.
  • Click-to-copy outputs HEX, ready for CSS and most design tools.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a shade and a tint?

A shade mixes the base with black, making it darker; a tint mixes it with white, making it lighter. Both keep the base colour's hue.

How many steps does the palette include?

The generator produces an evenly spaced ramp of shades and tints around your base, enough to cover the usual roles in an interface or document.

Can I export the whole palette at once?

Each swatch copies individually on click, which keeps the workflow simple and works with every tool that accepts pasted colour values.

Why do my ramp colours look different on another screen?

Displays vary in calibration and profile. The values are exact; judge final colours on the devices your audience actually uses.

Can I start from a colour in another format?

Yes. Convert it with the Color Converter first, or paste the HEX form directly as the base.

Is anything uploaded?

No. Palette generation happens entirely in your browser.

How many ramp steps does a project need?

Enough to cover the roles: typically a handful of shades and tints around the base. More steps offer flexibility; fewer steps enforce consistency. Start small and add only when a real need appears.

Can I generate a palette from a photo?

Not in this tool, which works from a single colour. Extract a dominant colour from the photo first, then generate its ramp; that two-step flow covers most photo-derived palettes.

Can I use the palette for print work?

The values are screen-space RGB-derived. For print, convert your chosen steps with a print-production tool that applies the correct colour profile, since screens and printers use different colour models.
